# Solar Noir — art direction

**The movement: Solar Noir.** Not a dark genre pastiche, but a study in high-key sunlight and long cool shadow. A coastal metropolis photographed at the last generous hour of afternoon, when ivory paving turns warm, terracotta facades glow, glass towers throw bright clean reflections, and the harbour holds a flat sheet of turquoise. The camera is a patient observer: it frames the city as a postcard that happens to be alive. Every surface is considered, every silhouette is placed with the painstaking attention of a master matte painter; the result must feel meticulously crafted from the first frame, the product of deep expertise rather than assembly.

**Palette as material, not decoration.** Chart-paper ivory `#e8ddc4` is the ground and the light — the pavement, the stucco, the haze that softens distance. Terracotta `#c96f4a` and coral `#ff6b4a` are the built warmth: facades, awnings, roof tiles, the red of a tail-light. Sea-glass `#5ce0c6` is the harbour and its reflections, a cool counterweight that makes the warm city read warmer. Glass blue `#7fb8c9` is the vertical city — towers that catch the low sun in clean bands. Deep shadow `#071a24` appears only in the long cool shadows cast across the ivory, giving the scene its graphic spine. Sun gold `#ffd166` is the single accent of consequence: the low sun itself, and the mission beam that rises above the boulevard. No colour is wasted; the restraint is the craft.

**The one image.** A wide sunlit boulevard runs straight toward the harbour. Palms lean over ivory paving, long shadows rake across the road, traffic crosses the frame in the near lane, glass towers flash behind, and above the waterline one gold beam of light marks the next job. That photograph is the whole game in a single frame: city, motion, objective, mood. Every screenshot should be composed to echo it — a strong receding line, a warm/cool split, and a small unambiguous gold vertical.

**Space, rhythm, craft.** The city is built from an honest grid: nine by nine blocks of 160 metres, roads cut between them at exact intervals, sidewalks as a clean margin, the harbour occupying the south-east quarter. Repetition is the point — thousands of windows, hundreds of palms, a patient rhythm of identical modules varied only where the eye needs rest. Parks break the grid, plazas open it, piers extend it into water. Instancing and shared geometry make that repetition possible in software rendering without sacrificing the illusion of a hand-built place. Buildings wear their age in drawn brick and window grids; cars are low, readable silhouettes, each kind distinct at a glance; pedestrians are small blocks with a gait. The work should look as though someone laboured over every tile and every frond, then stepped back and removed anything that did not belong.

**The eight districts.** Marisol Rows, Palma Vista, Puerto Alto, Coral Causeway, Old Customs House, Solano Plaza, Glass Mile, Seawall South. Each carries a colour and a role in the composition: the Rows are dense domestic warmth, Palma Vista is leafy and pale, Puerto Alto faces the water, the Causeway is open and sun-struck, the Old Customs House is civic and heavy, Solano Plaza is the bright public heart, Glass Mile is the vertical reflection, and Seawall South is where the city meets the sea.

**The four people.** Nia Vale, the courier who knows every shortcut; Rafa Solis, the broker who trades in favours; Ines Marlow, the harbour contact with a van that is not hers to give; Ada Cruz, the detective who waits at the plaza. Their dialogue is short, plain and spoken — names and need, never exposition. Text is sparse throughout: a line of mission, a number for money, a hint at the edge of the screen. Information lives in the world — in the gold beam, the red/blue flash, the minimap dot — not in paragraphs.

**Three anti-goals.** No neon: the palette stays high-key, sunlit and clean; night, glow sticks and electric signage are the opposite of this picture. No night: the light is always late-afternoon gold, the shadows long and cool, the sky pale and hazy. No real brands: nothing from a commercial franchise, no real marques, logos, characters, map or music — every name, facade and story here is original. The discipline of what is refused is what makes the remaining image feel authored rather than generated.
